# Sources & Corrections

This document records what was changed between v1 and v2 of *The Cycle*, and why. It exists so the content can be trusted and independently checked.

---

## Summary of corrections

The first version repeated several figures and framings that are common online but not well supported by current evidence. Here is what changed.

### 1. "Narcissistic abuse" reframed as a pattern, not a diagnosis
**v1:** Used "narcissist" and "narcissistic abuse" as though describing a clinical category.
**v2:** Added clear framing that "narcissistic abuse" is *not* a formal diagnosis. The DSM-5-TR describes conditions within individuals, not patterns of interpersonal harm. Abuse is a pattern of coercive control — a set of choices — and most people who abuse do not have a diagnosable personality disorder. You do not need to diagnose anyone to recognise harm.
**Why:** This is the current clinical consensus and the most important correction. Over-focusing on whether someone "is a narcissist" can distract from the pattern of behaviour, accountability, and safety — and the surge of unqualified online "experts" has muddied the clinical meaning.

### 2. Statistics corrected
- **"60 million Americans in narcissistic relationships" — REMOVED.** No rigorous epidemiological basis.
- **"78% develop C-PTSD" — REMOVED.** Traced to a non-peer-reviewed preprint; not a reliable figure.
- **NPD prevalence "6.2%" → "0.5–6%" range.** The 6.2% figure is the *high end*, from one 2008 survey (NESARC Wave 2, n=34,653) using broad criteria. The DSM-5-TR and most clinical sources estimate lower (~0.5–5%). Now shown as a range with a footnote.
- **"7 times to leave" → "~7×, commonly cited" with a footnote.** This figure is widely repeated by advocacy organisations but comes from helpline-collated data, not a peer-reviewed study; some sources cite 8. Reframed as illustrative.

### 3. The "self-hatred behind the mask" explanation marked as a model
**v1:** Presented the self-hatred/shame engine as established fact for all abusers.
**v2:** Framed as one psychodynamic lens that fits *some* people (especially those with strong narcissistic traits) and explicitly noted it does not describe every abuser — some feel little inner conflict.
**Why:** It is a useful and well-known theory (Kohut, Kernberg; supported in parts by the vulnerable-narcissism literature) but not a settled, universal fact.

### 4. Neurochemistry claims softened
**v1:** "Neurochemically indistinguishable from a drug"; "withdrawal identical to substance detox."
**v2:** Describes the reward/bonding systems (dopamine, oxytocin, cortisol) as *overlapping with* addiction pathways, and notes the chemistry is real but more complex and context-dependent than any single comparison.
**Why:** The overlap is genuine and well-documented, but recent reviews emphasise its complexity. "Identical" overstates it.

### 5. "He won't/can't change" softened
**v1:** Near-absolute claims about inability to change.
**v2:** Notes some people who harm do change through sustained voluntary work and accountability, while being honest that change is not produced by a partner tolerating more.
**Why:** More accurate and avoids both false hope and false hopelessness.

## Key sources

- Dutton, D. & Painter, S. (1981). "Traumatic Bonding: The development of emotional attachments in battered women and other relationships of intermittent abuse." *Victimology.* — origin of the term.
- Carnes, P. (1997). *The Betrayal Bond: Breaking Free of Exploitive Relationships.*
- National Domestic Violence Hotline (thehotline.org) — "Narcissism vs. Abuse," "50 Obstacles to Leaving."

**On NPD and prevalence**
- Stinson, F. S., et al. (2008). NESARC Wave 2 — lifetime NPD prevalence 6.2% (7.7% men, 4.8% women), n=34,653. *J. Clinical Psychiatry.*
- StatPearls (NCBI Bookshelf), "Narcissistic Personality Disorder" — community estimates range 0%–6.2%.
- DSM-5-TR (American Psychiatric Association, 2022) — NPD criteria; lower prevalence estimates.
- Weinberg & Ronningstam (2022) — clinical-population rates.

**On trauma, attachment, and neurobiology**
- van der Kolk, B. (2014). *The Body Keeps the Score.*
- Bowlby, J. (1969). *Attachment and Loss.*
- Reviews on oxytocin–dopamine interaction in bonding and addiction (2024–2025), e.g. work synthesised in *Frontiers in Human Neuroscience* and related journals — note the context-dependence of these systems.

**On the clinical critique of "narcissistic abuse" as a label**
- The Center for Growth (2026), "Not Everyone's a Narcissist."
- British Psychological Society, *The Psychologist* (2025) — interview on the risks of over-focusing on the narcissist.

- WomensLaw.org — "Is narcissism a type of emotional abuse?"

## A note on the spirit of the content

The goal is to be *both* emotionally true and factually careful. People reading this are often in pain and looking to have their experience named — that naming is genuinely helpful. The corrections above do not soften the reality of the harm; they make the guide more trustworthy, which matters most for exactly the readers it is written for.

*For education only. Not a substitute for professional care. If anything here conflicts with advice from your own therapist or advocate, follow theirs.*

## Addendum — Part II sourcing (comorbidity / addiction & blame)

The section on a survivor's own struggles being weaponized is grounded in:

- **Warshaw, Lyon, Phillips, et al. (2014), National Center on Domestic Violence, Trauma & Mental Health** — coined and documented "substance-use coercion." Source of the figure that 43% of surveyed female survivors reported a partner coercing them through substance use, and that a majority of abusers discouraged or sabotaged attempts to get sober.
- **Freyd, J. (1997); Harsey & Freyd (2020); Harsey, Zurbriggen & Freyd (2017)** — DARVO (Deny, Attack, Reverse Victim & Offender) and its empirical link to increased victim self-blame and reduced victim credibility with observers.
- **Khantzian (1985, 1997)** — the self-medication hypothesis; trauma/PTSD typically precedes substance use disorder, with substances used to regulate distress.
- **Reviews on co-occurring PTSD + SUD (e.g. ISTSS Clinician's Corner; systematic reviews 2011–2025)** — establishing the bidirectional relationship and that **integrated, trauma-informed treatment** (addressing both together) is the current standard of care and outperforms treating either alone.
- **"Reactive defence" framing** — increasingly preferred by clinicians over "reactive abuse," to avoid mislabeling a survival response to sustained provocation as abuse.

These are presented in the app as **education and self-support**, not as legal, medical, or custody advice. The app repeatedly directs readers to DV advocates and SAMHSA precisely because credibility and custody threats around substance use need qualified, individualized help.

## Addendum — The Undertow (self-check tool) accuracy review

**What it's grounded in.** The tool's core claim — that leaving an abusive relationship is a cyclical process, with a common "return" point and usually several separations before a final one — is directly supported by the Stages of Change literature applied to intimate-partner violence:
- Prochaska & DiClemente — Transtheoretical (Stages of Change) Model.
- Wuest & Merritt-Gray (1999), "Not Going Back: Sustaining the Separation in the Process of Leaving Abusive Relationships" — four stages: counteracting abuse, breaking free, not going back, moving on.
- Khaw & Hardesty (2009), *Journal of Family Theory & Review* — boundary ambiguity in the leaving process; documents the cyclical/discontinuous trajectory.

**The early-warning ordering** (mood → thinking → action) and the term **"euphoric recall"** are adapted from relapse-prevention research: Gorski & Miller (1986) outlined 11 warning-sign stages, which **Melemis (2015,** *Yale Journal of Biology and Medicine* 88(3):325–332**)** distilled into three (emotional → mental → physical).

**Corrections made during review:**
1. **Removed the shaming "relapse" framing.** The National DV Hotline explicitly cautions against comparing a return to an abuser to "relapse," because it can add guilt/shame. The tool now uses neutral "mood/thinking/action shift" language and states plainly that returning is common and not a failure.
2. **Fixed the attribution.** Earlier draft credited the three-stage model loosely to "Gorski; Melemis." Correct lineage: Gorski & Miller's 11 stages → Melemis (2015) three-stage distillation.
3. **Stopped implying the substance-relapse model was validated for relationship return.** It's used as a structural aid for *early warning signs only*; the cyclical-return claim is now carried by the IPV-specific Stages of Change sources above.
4. **Kept "an urge is a wave that crests and falls" guidance** (urge-surfing / distract-and-delay), which is standard in relapse-prevention and mindfulness-based relapse prevention.

## Full sweep corrections — all four files (2026)

### Most important scientific addition: the two necessary conditions

The most consistent gap across all four apps was the absence of what Dutton & Painter
(1981, 1993) identified as the TWO necessary conditions for traumatic bonding to form:

1. **A power imbalance** — financial, social, emotional, or physical control that narrows
   the victim's options and makes exit costly or dangerous.
2. **Intermittent reinforcement** — the unpredictable alternation of warmth and harm that
   trains the nervous system to chase relief from the same source as the pain.

Both must be present. This matters because it reframes the bond from "something that
just happens between two people in a bad relationship" to a structural condition that is
deliberately maintained — which is consistent with Stark's coercive-control framework
(2007) and with the current reconceptualisation of trauma bonding as intentional
reinforcement and domination, not merely a victim's psychological response
(Lesiak, cited in Wikipedia/Traumatic bonding, 2024 revision).

All four apps now include both conditions wherever the mechanism is explained.

### Neurochemistry framing corrected

The three-system framing now used across all apps is grounded in:
> "The dopamine-related reward/reinforcement/habit formation system, the
> oxytocin-related affiliation system, and the glucocorticoid-related stress response
> system." (Noriega-Reyes & Blumberg, PMC6857543, 2019)

Phrases like "neurochemically indistinguishable from a drug" and "identical to
substance detox" — which were in earlier versions — have been removed from all files.
The current phrasing is "activating overlapping neurobiological systems" and "described
by researchers as overlapping with the brain's addiction pathways."

### NPD model qualified: vulnerable vs grandiose narcissism

The "cruelty = self-hatred projected outward" explanation is now qualified in all files:
- In the *vulnerable* narcissism pattern, empirical research confirms shame mediates
  the link between narcissistic vulnerability and trait aggression (Kohut/Kernberg model;
  confirmed in multiple path-model studies including PMC9709537).
- In the *grandiose* pattern, cruelty can be more deliberate and less shame-driven.
Both patterns produce harm that originates in the perpetrator, not the victim.

### Returning is normal — de-shaming across all files

All files now state explicitly that multiple separations before a final exit is the
*expected, documented* pattern, consistent with:
- Wuest & Merritt-Gray (1999), "Not Going Back"
- Khaw & Hardesty (2009)
- National DV Hotline guidance against using "relapse" framing for return

The "7 times" statistic is presented where it appears as advocacy data (helpline-
collated), not a peer-reviewed finding.

### Attribution corrections

  both cited throughout.
- Melemis (2015), Yale J. Biol. Med. — three-stage relapse model (distilled from
  Gorski & Miller, 1986) — cited in TheUndertow only, where the framework is used.
