Before anything else

Read This First

You are reading this because a clear-headed version of you knew a foggy version might come back. Listen to the clear one.

If you're reading this because you feel the pull to go back — stop here and breathe.

The pull you feel right now is real. But real is not the same as true. Research on traumatic bonding identifies two conditions that built this pull: a power imbalance that narrowed your options, and intermittent reinforcement that trained your nervous system to chase relief from the same source as the pain. What feels like love right now is often those two things working as designed — not a signal to go back.

You did not leave because you were confused. You are confused now because you left. That's the cycle working exactly as designed — and it does not mean you were wrong.

You don't have to decide anything in this moment. You just have to not act yet. Read the rest. Then wait.

01
Keep this where you can find it

The Card

If you only have ten seconds, this is the part to keep. Screenshot it. Set it as a note. Tape it inside a cabinet.

When the pull comes, before I respond:

  1. I will not reply for at least 48 hours. Nothing real falls apart in 48 hours.
  2. I will call or text one safe person before I call them.
  3. I will re-read my own reasons for leaving (further down).
  4. I will remember: the warmth always came back — right before it disappeared again. That alternation was the mechanism, not the exception.
  5. This feeling will pass whether or not I go back. Going back is the only option that can't be undone.
02
The return — "hoovering"

Is It Happening Again?

The pull back in rarely looks like danger. It looks like hope. Tap each one that's happening — seeing them counted can cut through the fog.

  • A sudden, sweeping apologyEspecially one that sounds like the words you always wanted — but with no real change behind it.
  • "I've changed / I'm getting help now"Possible — some people do change through sustained voluntary work. But real change shows up over months of action, not in a message sent the week you left. The warmth you feel right now is Phase 1, not proof of change.
  • A crisis or emergencySuddenly they're sick, in trouble, or "the only person who can help is you." Manufactured need pulls on your compassion.
  • Nostalgia & "remember when"Flooding you with the good memories from Phase 1 — the version of them that you fell for, who rarely stayed.
  • Messages through other peopleMutual friends, family, or children carrying word that they "miss you" or "aren't doing well."
  • Perfect timingContact that lands on a holiday, an anniversary, a hard day they know about — when you're most likely to be lonely.
  • "Just one conversation"A request that sounds so small it feels unreasonable to refuse. The door only has to open a crack.
  • Pity, threats, or guilt"I can't live without you." "Look what you're doing to me." "After everything I did for you." Different tactics, same goal: reopen the door.
  • Love-bombing, round twoGifts, grand gestures, intensity that feels like the very beginning again. It felt like that the first time too.
  • Showing up uninvitedAt your door, your work, your usual places. (If this is happening and it frightens you, skip to the Safety section.)
03
Naming the fog

The Feeling Is Not The Fact

When the longing hits, your mind hands you an explanation that points back toward them. Here's what's usually on the other side of it.

What it feels like

"I miss them so much it hurts. That must mean it was love."

What's likely true

Missing someone and being safe with them are different things. Withdrawal hurts. The ache is real; it isn't evidence.

What it feels like

"Maybe I overreacted. Maybe it wasn't that bad."

What's likely true

Minimising is how you survived it. The distance is letting the fog clear — not exaggerating things. Trust what you wrote down when you were clear.

What it feels like

"They seem so different now. People can change."

What's likely true

People can. But you've seen this exact warmth before — it's Phase 1, and it has always returned to Phase 2. Change is measured in months of action, not a moment of charm.

What it feels like

"No one else will ever understand me like they did."

What's likely true

That belief was constructed through systematic isolation — a documented tactic that serves the power imbalance the bond depends on. It is not a fact about your future.

What it feels like

"If I just explain it right this time, they'll finally get it."

What's likely true

You have explained it, many times. The problem was never your words. One more conversation is the hook, not the solution.

You are not going back to the person who hurt you. You're going back to the hope of the person they pretended to be. That person was never available to stay.

Your own voice, for later

My Reasons

Write these now, while you can see clearly. This is you, leaving a message for the version of you who forgets.

Be specific. Specific memories cut through fog better than general ones. "The night of ___." "How I felt when ___." Your future self will need the details, because the fog erases them first.

05
A plan made in advance

If They Reach Out

Decide now what you'll do, so you don't have to decide in the flood of the moment. This is the plan.

Don't respond right away

The urge to reply instantly is the pull, not you. Put the phone down. Nothing genuine is lost by waiting. Set a timer if it helps — 48 hours is a good floor.

Tell one safe person immediately

Before you do anything else, tell someone you trust: "They reached out. I'm not okay right now." Saying it out loud breaks the secrecy the cycle depends on.

Re-read your own reasons

Scroll up to what you wrote in "My Reasons." That's the clear-headed you, talking to the you that's wavering. Believe that one.

Ask the harder question

Not "do I miss them?" — you will. Ask: "If nothing about them has actually changed, what would going back cost me?" You already know the answer.

If you must communicate, keep it flat

For unavoidable contact (co-parenting, shared logistics): keep it short, factual, unemotional — sometimes called "grey rock." No feelings to feed on, no opening to widen. Boring is safe.

Let the wave pass

The intensity of wanting to respond peaks and then falls, every time, usually within hours. You don't have to fight it forever. You just have to outlast this one wave.

06
This part matters most

Staying Safe

The time around leaving can be the most dangerous. Planning ahead is not paranoia — it's wisdom. This is not about fear; it's about being ready.

Practical safety steps

  • Trust escalation as a signal. If contact is turning to anger, threats, or showing up uninvited, that is information. Take it seriously even if part of you wants to minimise it.
  • Keep a record. Save threatening messages, log unwanted contact with dates. You don't have to act on it now, but documentation protects you later.
  • Tell people what's happening. A few trusted people knowing your situation is a safety net — at home, at work, wherever you spend time.
  • Know your exits. If you share space or must meet, think through how you'd leave and where you'd go. Keep essentials (keys, phone, ID, a little cash) reachable.
  • A hotline is not only for emergencies. Advocates help with safety planning, options, and just thinking it through — free and confidential, before anything escalates.
  • If you are ever in immediate danger, call 911. Your safety comes before any plan on this page.

07
Who you call instead

My People

When the pull says "call them," this is who you call instead. Fill it in now, so the names are ready before you need them.

Aim for at least two or three. Different people for different moments — one who calms you, one who'll come over, one who'll tell you the truth. You don't have to carry this alone, and you were never meant to.

Saved.

Leaving isn't one decision. It's the same decision, made again and again, until one day it stays made. Research on leaving abusive relationships confirms that multiple separations before a final exit is the normal pattern — not a sign of failure.

Every time you don't go back is a win — even the times it was hard, even the times you almost did.
