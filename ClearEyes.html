01
The four phases

The Cycle

Four phases, repeating. Each turn tightens the bond and makes the last one feel further away than it was.

A note on language before we begin

This guide uses words like "narcissistic abuse" because that's how many people first find their experience named — and being named matters. But know this: "narcissistic abuse" is not a clinical diagnosis, and most people who abuse do not have a diagnosable personality disorder. Abuse is a pattern of coercive control — a series of choices — not a medical condition.

Which means: you don't need to diagnose anyone to recognise harm and act on it.

Phase 1

Idealize

Love Bombing

Overwhelming affection and praise. They reflect your best qualities back at you — you feel profoundly seen. That feeling is real. What isn't real is that they caused it; you were, in part, falling for your own goodness reflected back.

"I've never felt this understood." — You're experiencing your own light shown back to you.
Phase 2

Devalue

Criticism, cold, contempt

Warmth is withdrawn without warning. Criticism begins, then escalates. You start asking what you did wrong. The answer is nothing — but the question itself is the point. You're now doing double work: managing your pain and theirs.

"What did I do? I've been trying so hard." — The confusion is the mechanism, not a side effect.
Phase 3

Discard

Withdrawal, abandonment

They leave, go silent, or threaten to. The threat of abandonment fires your deepest survival wiring — the brain treats it like physical danger. Not weakness: neurobiology doing exactly what it evolved to do.

"I can't breathe without them." — What feels like love here is a nervous system in alarm.
Phase 4

Hoover

The return

They come back — apologies, promises, the person you first met. Relief floods you — dopamine (reward), oxytocin (bonding), and cortisol (stress dropping) shift at once. Researchers describe this as activating the same neurobiological systems (reward, affiliation, stress regulation) that are disrupted in substance dependence. The cycle restarts, deeper — the intermittency itself strengthens the bond each time (Dutton & Painter, 1993).

"This is the real them. It'll be different now." — It felt exactly like this last time, too.

The cycle isn't random. Each phase has a function. Research identifies two necessary conditions for trauma bonding: a power imbalance (financial, social, emotional, or physical control) and intermittent reinforcement (the unpredictable alternation of warmth and harm). Both must be present. Together they build a dependency that overlaps with the brain's reward, bonding, and stress systems — which is why logic alone rarely breaks it. (Dutton & Painter, 1981, 1993)

02
The engine

Why It Happens

When the pattern fits this picture, the cruelty has a source. Understanding it doesn't excuse it — it removes you from the centre of it.

One explanation, not the only one

The "self-hatred behind the mask" picture below comes from psychodynamic theory and fits some abusive people well, especially those with strong narcissistic traits. It's a useful lens, not a settled fact, and doesn't describe everyone. Some who harm feel little inner conflict at all. Either way, the cause lives in them, not in you.

Why are they so cruel?
In the vulnerable narcissism pattern, empirical research confirms shame mediates the link between narcissistic vulnerability and aggression — the person cannot hold self-hatred inward and directs it outward. In the grandiose pattern, cruelty can be more deliberate and less shame-driven. Either way: the cruelty originates in them, and was never earned by you.
Why build you up, then tear you down?
When you reflect their idealised self, they're warm. When you start showing them something true, they can't bear it. They attack the mirror, not the woman.
Why do they keep coming back?
Often not for you, but for what you provide: validation that quiets the self-hatred, a reflection they can control. They come back for the supply, and may not know the difference.
Why is nothing you do ever enough?
Because the problem was never your performance. It lives inside them, and predates you. You were never the cause, and could never be the cure.

The Blame, The Guilt & Your Own Struggles
If you've been told you're the real problem — the drinker, the unstable one, the reason it all happens — this part is for you.
03
"But maybe I really am the problem"

When You're Carrying Your Own Struggles

Drinking. Using. Depression. Anger that scares you. Memory that won't hold. If you have struggles of your own, you may have been told they are the reason for everything. Let's look at that honestly — and fairly.

Here is the truth that abuse makes almost impossible to hold: two things can be real at the same time. You can have a genuine problem with alcohol or drugs — one that deserves real care — and be the target of abuse that you did nothing to earn. Neither one cancels the other. Neither one is permission for the other.

An abusive partner needs you to believe these two facts are the same fact. They need "you drink" to mean "so this is your fault." It does not. A drinking problem is something to heal. It is never a reason to be controlled, frightened, or harmed.

True: you may have a real problem

Your substance use, your depression, your reactions — these can be real and worth addressing with honest, compassionate help. Naming them isn't agreeing with your abuser; it's taking your own life back.

— and at the same time —

True: it does not justify the abuse

Nothing about your struggles makes you deserve fear, control, or harm. There is no amount of drinking that earns abuse. The harm was a choice someone else made — every single time.

"I wasn't a perfect partner" is true of every human being alive. It is not the same as "I caused the abuse." Hold the first with honesty. Put the second down — it was never yours.

They didn't hurt you because you drank. Many people drink and are never harmed for it. They hurt you because they chose to — and your struggle just gave them a story that let them keep choosing it.
04
Substance-use coercion & DARVO

How Your Struggle Gets Weaponized

This has a name. Researchers call it "substance-use coercion" — a recognised tactic of control. Once you see the moves, they lose some of their grip. Here's what it sounds like, and what's really happening underneath.

You're a drunk — no one will ever believe you.
What's really happening

This is about credibility. By making your struggle the headline, they make sure that if you ever reach out — to police, family, a court — your word can be dismissed. Undermining your believability is a documented control tactic, not an accident.

Look what you made me do. If you weren't like this, I wouldn't have to.
What's really happening

This is DARVO — Deny, Attack, Reverse Victim & Offender. They deny the harm, attack your stability, and flip the roles so you become the offender. Research links DARVO directly to increased self-blame in the person being harmed. The guilt you feel is the tactic working, not evidence that it's deserved.

Here, have another. Let's just drink and forget the fight.
What's really happening

Encouraging or pushing your use keeps you easier to control — less able to plan, remember clearly, or leave. Studies report that a majority of abusers actively discourage or sabotage attempts to get sober, because your dependence serves them.

You don't even remember what happened. You're making it up.
What's really happening

If substances or trauma have affected your memory, that gap becomes a weapon — a place to insert their version of reality. This is gaslighting aimed at the seam where you're most vulnerable. A shaky memory of an event does not mean the harm didn't happen.

I'll tell them you're an unfit mother. They'll take the kids.
What's really happening

Threats to use your struggle against you in custody or with authorities are a way to trap you in place. They are frightening precisely because they hold a grain of plausibility — which is what makes documentation and an advocate so important (see Part Four).

If you ever lashed back

Many people who are cornered eventually shout, throw something, or hit back — and are then told they are the abuser. Clinicians increasingly call this a reactive defence, not abuse: a survival response to sustained provocation. It can feel like proof you're "just as bad." It isn't. A single defensive reaction inside a long pattern of control is not the same as the pattern itself.

05
Why the drinking grew

The Pain Loop — and How It Breaks

If your use got worse during the relationship, there's a reason that has nothing to do with weakness. It's one of the most studied patterns in trauma psychology: the self-medication loop.

1

The harm — fear, humiliation, chronic stress with no safe outlet.

↓
2

The numbing — a drink quiets the alarm. For a moment, you can breathe. (Research calls this self-medication; trauma usually comes first, the substance after.)

↓
3

The blame — "See? You're the drunk. This is you." The use becomes their evidence, and your shame.

↓
4

More harm, more shame — which makes the next drink make more sense. The loop tightens.

Here's the hopeful part the shame hides from you: because the trauma and the substance are linked, treating them together works. The current standard of care is integrated, trauma-informed treatment — addressing the pain and the substance use as one connected thing, not two separate failures. You don't have to "get clean first to deserve help with the abuse," or vice versa. Both, together, is the path that the evidence supports.

You are not a bad person with a drinking problem. You may be a hurt person who found the only relief that was available. That reframe isn't an excuse — it's the doorway to the right kind of help.

Getting honest about your own struggles and refusing to carry the blame for someone else's cruelty are not opposites. They're the same act of self-respect.

06
Before anything else

When The Pull Comes Back

You're reading this because a clear-headed version of you knew a foggy version might return. Listen to the clear one.

If you feel the pull to go back — stop here and breathe.

The pull is real. But real is not the same as true. Research on traumatic bonding identifies two conditions that created this pull: a power imbalance that narrowed your options, and intermittent reinforcement that trained your nervous system to chase relief from the same source as the pain. What feels like love right now is often those two things working as designed.

You did not leave because you were confused. You're confused now because you left. That's the cycle working as designed — not proof you were wrong.

You don't have to decide anything this moment. You just have to not act yet. Read on. Then wait.

07
Keep this where you'll find it

The Card

If you only have ten seconds, keep this. Screenshot it. Tape it inside a cabinet.

When the pull comes, before I respond:

  1. I won't reply for at least 48 hours. Nothing real falls apart in 48 hours.
  2. I'll contact one safe person before I contact them.
  3. I'll re-read my own reasons for leaving (below).
  4. I'll remember the warmth always came back — right before it vanished again.
  5. This feeling passes whether or not I go back. Going back is the only choice that can't be undone.
  6. My drinking (or struggle) is not a reason to return. Their story about it was never the truth.
08
The return — "hoovering"

Is It Happening Again?

The pull back in rarely looks like danger. It looks like hope. Tap each one that's happening — seeing them counted can cut the fog.

  • A sweeping apologyThe words you always wanted — with no real change behind them.
  • "I've changed / I'm getting help now"Possible — some people do change through sustained voluntary work. Real change shows up over months to years of action, not in a message sent the week you left. The warmth right now is Phase 1 again, not evidence of change.
  • A sudden crisisThey're sick, in trouble, "only you can help." Manufactured need pulls on your compassion.
  • "Remember when"Flooding you with Phase 1 memories — the version of them that rarely stayed.
  • Messages through other peopleFriends, family, or your children carrying word that they "miss you."
  • Perfect timingContact landing on a holiday, anniversary, or a hard day they know about.
  • Using your struggle as a hook"You'll relapse without me." "You can't cope alone." Turning your vulnerability into a reason to return.
  • Pity, threats, or guilt"I can't live without you." "Look what you're doing to me." Different tactics, same goal: reopen the door.
  • Love-bombing, round twoGifts, intensity, the very beginning again. It felt like that the first time too.
  • Showing up uninvitedAt your door, work, your usual places. (If this frightens you, go to Safety.)
09
Naming the fog

The Feeling Is Not The Fact

When the longing hits, your mind hands you an explanation pointing back toward them. Here's what's usually on the other side.

Feels like

"I miss them so much it hurts. That must mean it was love."

Likely true

Missing someone and being safe with them are different things. Withdrawal hurts. The ache is real; it isn't evidence.

Feels like

"Maybe I overreacted. Maybe it wasn't that bad."

Likely true

Minimising is how you survived. Distance is letting the fog clear — not exaggerating. Trust what you knew when you were clear.

Feels like

"They were right — I'm a mess. Who else would put up with me?"

Likely true

That belief was constructed through systematic isolation — a deliberate tactic that maintained the power imbalance the bond depended on. Your struggles are real and workable; they do not make you unlovable or unworthy of safety.

Feels like

"If I just explain it right this time, they'll finally get it."

Likely true

You've explained it many times. The problem was never your words. One more conversation is the hook, not the solution.

You're not going back to the person who hurt you. You're going back to the hope of the person they pretended to be. That person was never available to stay.

11
A plan made in advance

If They Reach Out

Decide now what you'll do, so you don't have to decide in the flood. This is the plan.

Don't respond right away

The urge to reply instantly is the pull, not you. Urges peak and then fall — usually within 20–30 minutes to a few hours — whether or not you act. 48 hours is a good floor for any decision about contact. Nothing genuine is lost by waiting.

Tell one safe person immediately

"They reached out. I'm not okay right now." Saying it out loud breaks the secrecy the cycle depends on.

Re-read your own reasons

Scroll up to "My Reasons." That's the clear you, talking to the wavering you. Believe that one.

Name the hook if it's about your struggle

If the message is "you'll fall apart without me," recognise it: your recovery is safer away from the person who profited from your pain. Lean on real support, not them.

If you must communicate, keep it flat

For unavoidable contact (co-parenting, logistics): short, factual, unemotional — "grey rock." No feelings to feed on, no opening to widen. Boring is safe.

Let the wave pass

The urge peaks and falls, every time, usually within hours. You don't have to fight it forever — just outlast this one wave.

12
This part matters most

Staying Safe

The time around leaving can be the most dangerous. Planning ahead isn't paranoia — it's wisdom.

Practical safety steps

  • Treat escalation as information. Anger, threats, or showing up uninvited — take it seriously even if part of you wants to minimise it.
  • Keep a record. Save threatening messages; log unwanted contact with dates. If they've lied about your substance use to others, documentation of their behaviour helps counter it later.
  • Get an advocate early — especially with custody or credibility threats. Domestic-violence advocates understand substance-use coercion and can help you prepare so your struggle isn't turned against you. You don't have to navigate the system alone.
  • Ask for trauma-informed, integrated care. If substance use is part of your story, look for help that treats trauma and substance use together — that's the current standard and it works better than tackling either alone.
  • Tell a few trusted people what's happening — a safety net at home, at work, wherever you spend time.
  • Know your exits. Keep essentials (keys, phone, ID, some cash, any medication) reachable.
  • If you're ever in immediate danger, call 911. Safety comes before any plan on this page.

Who you call instead

My People

When the pull says "call them," this is who you call instead. Fill it in now, so the names are ready before you need them.

Aim for two or three. Different people for different moments — one who calms you, one who'll come over, one who'll tell you the truth. If recovery is part of your path, a sponsor or counsellor belongs on this list too.
