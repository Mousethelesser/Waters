01
The Four Phases

The Cycle

Four phases. Repeating endlessly. Each cycle tightens the bond and makes the last one feel further away than it was.

A note on language before we begin

This guide uses the words "narcissistic abuse" because that is how many people first find their experience named — and being named matters. But it is worth knowing: "narcissistic abuse" is not a clinical diagnosis, and most people who abuse do not have a diagnosable personality disorder. Abuse is a pattern of coercive control — a series of choices — not a medical condition.

This matters for one practical reason: you do not need to diagnose anyone to recognise harm and act on it. Whether or not the person fits a label, the pattern below is real, the harm is real, and your response to it is valid.

Phase 1

Idealize

Love Bombing

Overwhelming affection, attention, and praise. He studies you and reflects your best qualities back at you — you feel profoundly seen. That feeling is real. What's not real is that he caused it. You were falling in love with yourself, reflected.

"I've never felt this understood before." — You are experiencing your own goodness shown back to you. He found the frequency and tuned to it.
Phase 2

Devalue

Criticism, Cold, Contempt

Without warning, warmth is withdrawn. Criticism begins — subtle at first, then escalating. You start asking what you did wrong. Answer: nothing. He cannot sustain the idealised reflection. When you start showing him something real, he attacks it.

"What did I do? I've been trying so hard." — You are now doing double work: managing your own pain AND managing his. This is by design.
Phase 3

Discard

Withdrawal, Abandonment

He leaves, goes silent, or threatens to. The terror of abandonment activates your deepest survival wiring. Your brain interprets the threat of losing him the same way it processes physical danger. This is not weakness — it is neurobiology doing exactly what it was built to do.

"I can't breathe without him. I'll do anything." — What feels like love at this stage is your nervous system in a threat response. The bond deepens precisely because the fear is real.
Phase 4

Hoover

The Return

He comes back. Apologies, promises, the man you first met. Your brain floods with relief — the same reward and bonding systems involved in dopamine and oxytocin signalling. Researchers describe this as overlapping with the brain's addiction pathways, which is part of why the pull feels so physical. (The chemistry is real but more complex than any single tidy comparison.) He is not choosing you. He is choosing what you provide.

"This is the real him. He came back. Things will be different." — The cycle restarts. Deeper than before. The bond is tightened each time.

The cycle is not random. Each phase serves a function. Together they produce a neurochemical dependency that is clinically identical to addiction — complete with tolerance, withdrawal, and compulsive return.

02
The Engine

Why He Does This

When the pattern fits this picture, the cruelty has a source. Understanding it doesn't excuse it — but it removes you from the centre of it.

One explanation, not the only one

The "self-hatred behind the mask" picture below comes from psychodynamic theory and describes one recognisable pattern — it fits some abusive people well, especially those with strong narcissistic traits. It is a useful lens, not a settled fact, and it does not describe every abuser. Some who harm feel little inner conflict at all. Either way, the cause lives in them, not in you.

Why is he so cruel?
Because he hates himself — and he cannot direct that hatred inward without collapsing. So he directs it outward, at the person closest to him. Every cruel word he gave you was aimed at himself. You were just the nearest surface.
Why does he build you up and then tear you down?
When you reflect his idealised self back at him, he loves you. When you start showing him something true — cracks, reality, the man behind the mask — he cannot bear it. He attacks the mirror, not the woman. The mirror just happened to be you.
Why does he come back every time?
Not for you. For what you provide: a reflection he can control, validation that temporarily quiets the self-hatred, a false self he can maintain in your presence. He comes back for the supply, not the person. He doesn't know the difference.
Why does nothing you do ever seem to be enough?
Because the problem was never your performance. He is running from his own internal reality. No amount of love, patience, or self-improvement can fix a problem that lives inside him. You were never the cause. You could never be the cure.
Does he know he's doing this?
It varies. Where this pattern is driven by deep shame, the defence is often partly unconscious — built early in life, maintained automatically. But many people who abuse are quite aware of choosing when, where, and toward whom they do it. Awareness or its absence does not change the harm, and it is not your job to work out which one you are dealing with.
03
What You Were to Him

Narcissistic Supply

You weren't his partner. You were his source. The distinction matters.

Narcissistic supply is the term for the external validation a narcissist requires to maintain his false self. Without it, the self-hatred breaks through. You were selected — not loved — because you were a high-quality source.

01

You were chosen for your light

Empathy, warmth, resilience, loyalty — qualities that made you an excellent mirror. He didn't fall in love with you. He fell in love with what your brightness showed him about himself.

02

Your empathy was the target

The very thing that makes you want to understand, forgive, and try harder is the thing that kept you in the cycle. Your capacity for compassion was the vulnerability that was exploited.

03

His coldness was withdrawal of supply

When he went cold, he wasn't punishing you for something real. He was withdrawing the resource he controlled to trigger your anxiety, recalibrate your behaviour, and restore the supply he needed.

04

You were never seen

Not because you weren't worth seeing — because he was incapable of it. Genuine seeing requires the ability to hold another person as separate from oneself. He could not do this. Every image he had of you was a projection.

The cruelty was never a reflection of your worth. It was a reflection of his wound — thrown outward because he had nowhere else to put it. — a recurring theme in trauma-informed therapy
04
The Impossible Task

Why You Can't Fix It

You have been trying to solve a problem that was never yours. Here is why it cannot be solved from where you are standing.

The wound predates you by decades
The narcissistic defence was built in childhood to survive experiences of shame, abandonment, or conditional love. You didn't create it. You cannot heal what you didn't wound.
He cannot access the wound with you present
Genuine healing requires a person to confront their own reality without the buffer of supply. As long as you are there providing validation, the motivation to do the work doesn't exist. Your presence enables avoidance of the very thing that would require change.
Loving harder makes the cycle tighter
Every time you stayed, forgave, and tried again, you taught the cycle that it works. Your love and patience did not soften him — they confirmed that the behaviour had no lasting consequences. Unconditional love in this dynamic is not a gift. It is fuel.
Change requires his choice, not your effort
Some people who harm others do change — through sustained, voluntary work and genuine accountability, usually over a long time. What the evidence does not support is change produced by a partner loving harder, staying longer, or tolerating more. You cannot want someone's healing more than they do and expect it to work.

Recognising this is not giving up on him. It is giving up the belief that his suffering is something you caused — or something you can carry.

05
The Exit

Why Leaving Feels Impossible

Not weakness. Wiring. There is a clinical reason you couldn't just leave — and it has nothing to do with how smart or strong you are.

  • Your brain wired him as survival. Intermittent reinforcement — unpredictable reward — creates stronger compulsive attachment than consistent love ever could. Your brain learned to chase his approval the way it chases oxygen.
  • He is both the poison and the pain relief. He creates the wound and then soothes it. Your nervous system cannot separate the source of pain from the source of comfort — because they are the same person. Leaving can trigger something that closely resembles substance withdrawal: real, physical, and temporary.
  • He became your reality. Years of gaslighting and isolation eroded your trust in your own perception. You stopped believing what you saw. His version of events became the only version available.
  • The departure window is the most dangerous time. The fear of leaving is not irrational. Statistically, the period of separation is when abuse escalates most sharply. Your fear was reading the evidence accurately.
  • You were trying to leave a person you loved. The man from Phase 1 was real to you — the warmth, the connection, the hope. Leaving required grieving someone who never fully existed. That grief is legitimate and it is not simple.

Seven attempts is the average number of times a person leaves before the final exit. Every attempt was brave. The ones that didn't stick were not failures — they were part of the process of becoming certain enough to go.

06
Recognition

Signs You Are In It

These are not character flaws. They are symptoms. A normal response to an abnormal situation.

  • You apologise constantly — often without knowing what for
  • You feel responsible for his moods and emotions
  • You walk on eggshells, reading the room before you speak
  • You make yourself smaller to avoid triggering his anger
  • You feel crazy — and he has confirmed that you are
  • You remember a version of him that no longer shows up consistently
  • You defend him to friends and family who are concerned
  • You have tried to leave and found yourself unable to stay gone
  • You believe that if you could just be better, things would return to how they were at the start
  • You feel more alone inside the relationship than you did before it
07
The One Question

The Answer

There is one question every person in this cycle eventually arrives at.

How do you love a man who hates himself?
You love yourself instead.
Not because he isn't worth loving.
Because you cannot save someone from themselves.
And you were never meant to try.

Choosing yourself is not abandonment. It is the recognition that you are also a person who deserves to be seen — not used as a surface for someone else's reflection.
